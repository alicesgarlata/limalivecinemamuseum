Documentation

Lima Live Cinema Museum is a digital exhibition about the relationship between cinema and the urban space of Lima. This page documents the research framework, collection criteria, editorial model, narrative structure, visual design and technical architecture developed for the project.

1. Concept and Scope

The project begins with a simple question: what can the locations chosen by filmmakers tell us about Lima? Rather than treating filming locations as isolated tourist attractions, Lima Live approaches them as evidence of how the city has been represented, remembered and transformed through cinema.

The collection brings together public spaces, streets, residential complexes, commercial areas, natural landscapes, transport infrastructure and historically significant sites. Their cinematic appearances reveal different versions of Lima: monumental and everyday, affluent and marginalised, intimate and political, coastal and metropolitan.

The current dataset contains 24 location records and 23 film records. The films represented in the wider collection range from 1930 to 2025, while the historical narrative begins in 1938 with Palomillas del Rímac. The collection is not intended as a complete catalogue of Peruvian cinema; it is a curated selection designed to support spatial exploration and historical interpretation.

2. Collection Criteria

Locations were selected by combining cinematographic, historical, geographic and interpretive criteria. Priority was given to places that could demonstrate a meaningful relationship between a film and the social or urban history of Lima.

  • Documented cinematic connection: the location is associated with one or more films represented in the dataset.
  • Historical or cultural relevance: the place contributes to understanding a significant period, event, community or transformation of the city.
  • Geographic variety: the collection includes different districts and types of urban space rather than concentrating exclusively on the historic centre or tourist areas.
  • Narrative value: the location can support comparison between films or become a meaningful stop in a historical or geographic itinerary.
  • Available evidence: sufficient information could be gathered about the place, the film and their relationship.

Locations for which the filming connection could not yet be sufficiently verified, or for which the available information was too limited, are not presented as definitive records. The collection can therefore be expanded as new evidence, films and images become available.

3. Content and Data Model

The exhibition is organised around two connected entities: locations and films. A location record contains its name, address, district, coordinates, accessibility information, associated films and multiple versions of its introductory text. Extended records add historical context, curiosities, cultural significance and sources. Film records contain title, year, director, country, genres, cast, production information, description and, when available, a Wikidata identifier.

This structure allows the same content to be explored in several ways: spatially through the map, by film through the collection catalogue, individually through location pages, chronologically through the historical timeline and geographically through the three itineraries.

4. Adaptive Text Design

Each location can contain a 3×3 grid of introductory texts. The grid is based on two independent axes rather than three:

  • Tone and complexity: Young, Adult and Professional.
  • Length: Brief, Mid and Long.

The “Too difficult” and “Too simple” controls move between the three tone levels, while “Too long” and “Too short” change the length. The interface therefore lets visitors adjust the same core information without leaving the location page. “Competence” is the interaction through which the tone axis is presented, not a third independent dimension.

The default combination is Adult + Mid. When a specific cell is unavailable, the interface falls back to the mid-length text for the selected tone and informs the visitor that the complete version is still in development.

5. Narratives and Itineraries

The Narratives section reorganises the collection into five curated paths. The first is interpretive and chronological; the other four can also function as real-world itineraries.

  • I. Historical Timeline — Lima on Screen: follows changes in the cinematic image of Lima from 1938 to 2024, moving from early urban modernity through political conflict, social crisis, memory and contemporary forms of representation.
  • II. Cinematic Miraflores: connects Kennedy Park, Tarata Street, Larco Avenue, the Miraflores Boardwalk and Pizza Street. The route contrasts tourism, everyday social life, political memory, the coastal landscape and nightlife.
  • III. San Isidro: connects El Olivar Forest, Country Club Lima Hotel and the Santa Cruz Residential Complex. The itinerary compares natural heritage, elite architecture and modern collective housing.
  • IV. Callao & La Punta: moves from Jorge Chávez International Airport to Monumental Del Callao, Cantolao Beach and the islands of Callao, including El Frontón. It links modernisation, port identity, neighbourhood memory, adolescence and political history.

Transition texts in the historical timeline connect individual films to broader changes in Peruvian society. Maps and numbered markers preserve the spatial dimension of each narrative, while links return visitors to the full records of the locations involved.

The itineraries are interpretive cultural routes rather than real-time navigation services. Transport, opening conditions, accessibility and restrictions may change and should be verified before an in-person visit.

6. Visual and Editorial Design

6.1 Design concept: a contemporary cinema museum

The visual identity was designed to distinguish Lima Live from a conventional tourism website. The interface does not present locations simply as destinations to consume; it treats them as records in a contemporary museum collection. Large editorial titles, restrained labels, generous spacing, image-led entrances and visible relationships between films and places establish an exhibition-oriented tone.

The contemporary mode uses an off-white paper-like ground, dark typography and a muted burgundy accent. Playfair Display gives titles an editorial and cinematic presence, while a neutral sans-serif keeps navigation, metadata and controls direct. The result combines the authority of a museum catalogue with the immediacy of a digital interface.

Spatial exploration and close reading are deliberately connected. The map offers an overview of the city; cards provide a first interpretive layer; individual location pages expand the record; the Films page reverses the perspective by beginning with a production; and Narratives turn selected records into chronological and geographic arguments.

6.2 Information architecture and page roles

Each page has a distinct communicative task, so the project uses a shared visual language without forcing every section into the same layout.

  • Home: introduces the project through a full-viewport cinematic hero, previews the three geographic itineraries and leads directly into the mapped collection.
  • Locations: combines search, district filters, a Leaflet map and a scrollable record column. It supports both targeted retrieval and open-ended spatial discovery.
  • Location record: begins with a large photographic header, then separates the main interpretive record from the associated-film column. Visitors can adjust text tone and length without changing page.
  • Films: presents a searchable and filterable catalogue. Opening a film in a dialog preserves the user’s position in the collection while revealing metadata, description and linked locations.
  • Narratives: uses a persistent route selector, long-form editorial text and maps to connect spatial evidence with historical interpretation.
  • Documentation, About and Disclaimer: use a wide editorial reading layout while separating research and design decisions, project identity and responsibilities, and the legal or practical limitations of the exhibition.

6.3 Functional grids and dimensions

The website does not rely on one universal twelve-column grid. Each page uses a functional grid based on the kind of material it presents. CSS Grid manages relationships between text, images and maps; Flexbox is used for one-dimensional groups such as navigation, filters, metadata and controls.

6.4 Proportional system and golden ratio

The contemporary interface uses the golden ratio as a flexible proportional guide rather than a rigid template. On wide screens, selected editorial grids divide available space approximately into 61.8% and 38.2%, establishing a clear relationship between primary and supporting content. The same progression informs the spacing scale of 16, 26, 42, 68 and 110 pixels. Film catalogue images use a 16:10 ratio, whose value of 1.6 closely approximates the golden ratio.

The ratio is applied to the homepage itinerary heading, the film catalogue heading and the editorial layouts used by Documentation, About and Disclaimer. It is not imposed on functional components such as the map, location metadata panels or responsive single-column layouts, where fixed proportions could reduce readability or usability. Responsive breakpoints therefore override the proportional grids whenever the available viewport becomes too narrow.

6.5 Image ratios and photographic treatment

Images are not decorative additions: they establish the relationship between the real city and its cinematic representation. Different ratios are used according to the function of the image rather than forcing every visual into one format.

  • Home hero: uses a fluid full-viewport background with background-size: cover. Some edge cropping is accepted because the image functions as an immersive atmosphere behind the exhibition title.
  • Location header: has no fixed aspect ratio. Its height scales with clamp(420px, 66svh, 720px), allowing the place to function as the visual identity of the record. A dark lower gradient, white title and layered shadow protect readability across photographs with different brightness levels.
  • Narrative photographs: use a 16:9 display ratio to recall the horizontal field of cinema and to keep consecutive chapters visually consistent.
  • Film catalogue and gallery images: use a 16:10 ratio, balancing a cinematic horizontal composition with the vertical space required by catalogue cards.
  • Film dialog: gives the image a separate visual column on desktop and moves it above the record on smaller screens, preserving the hierarchy between visual evidence and metadata.

Location photographs converted to WebP reduce transfer size without changing the underlying subject or editorial content. Theme-specific filters alter saturation, contrast, greyscale or sepia presentation, but do not replace the source image.

6.6 Responsive strategy

The interface uses fluid measurements and page-specific breakpoints rather than designing only for a small list of named devices. Functions such as clamp(), minmax(), viewport units and flexible gaps allow typography and spacing to scale continuously between minimum and maximum values.

  • At 1100 px the film catalogue changes from three columns to two.
  • At 980 px narrative maps stop being sticky and move below the text, avoiding an unusably narrow reading column.
  • At 900–820 px homepage and documentation grids become single-column layouts.
  • At 760 px the location record places its film column below the main text.
  • At 700 px the theme selector becomes a compact side tab with a panel sized to the available viewport.
  • At 620–520 px hero actions, itinerary previews, timelines, maps and spacing are simplified for portrait screens.

This strategy preserves the order and meaning of the content while changing its spatial arrangement. Desktop layouts support comparison and simultaneous viewing; portrait layouts prioritise a clear reading sequence and touch access.

6.7 Typography and hierarchy

The main interface uses scale contrast rather than a large number of decorative elements. Display titles identify the museum, places, films and narrative chapters; small uppercase labels identify metadata and controls; body text uses generous line-height for sustained reading.

6.8 Interaction design

  • Map and cards: markers and location cards are coordinated so that selecting one spatial representation activates the corresponding editorial record.
  • Search and filtering: free-text search, district pills, a clear control and a live result count make the map usable as a retrieval interface rather than only a visual illustration.
  • Progressive disclosure: short card descriptions lead to complete location pages; film cards open detailed dialogs; extended film profiles and location sections can be revealed when needed.
  • Adaptive reading: tone and length controls let visitors change how a location introduction is written while remaining on the same record and preserving the same underlying subject.
  • Narrative orientation: sticky tabs and numbered map markers maintain awareness of the active route and the sequence of stops.
  • Theme selection: the switcher begins as a small side tab, can move between the left and right edges and opens only on request, reducing obstruction of the map and page content.

6.9 Visual design system and required editorial styles

The historical and speculative styles were implemented in response to the course assignment, which required the selection of different editorial periods and the creation of alternative designs for the same website. The selected periods are therefore design references, not curatorial categories. They do not correspond to the release dates of the films, phases in the history of Lima or different interpretations of the collection.

The interface strictly separates collection data from visual presentation: films, locations, coordinates, photographs, associations and narrative texts remain unchanged when another style is selected. The alternatives modify only presentation variables and interface composition, including colours, typography, spacing, borders, card shapes, image and map filters, decorative patterns, animation and selected layout behaviours.

CSS custom properties control background, surface, ink, muted text, accents, borders, typography, radii, shadows and image or map filters. JavaScript applies the selected style through a data attribute and stores the choice in localStorage, allowing it to persist between pages and visits. This implementation demonstrates how one HTML and data structure can support multiple coherent visual systems without duplicating or semantically altering the content.

6.10 Accessibility within the visual system

Theme variation is constrained by usability requirements. Text and surfaces use paired design tokens, photographic location titles remain white over a strengthened dark gradient, and tags or badges receive explicit foreground and background combinations. Interactive controls use native buttons or links wherever possible, film cards expose a visible keyboard focus state, and layouts avoid requiring hover as the only way to reach content.

Decorative motion is disabled or reduced when prefers-reduced-motion is active. On smaller screens, sticky or side-by-side elements return to the normal document flow. This prevents the theme system from becoming more important than the collection it is intended to present.

7. Technical Architecture

Lima Live is a static website built with HTML, CSS and JavaScript and published through GitHub Pages. The static approach keeps the project portable, transparent and inexpensive to maintain, while avoiding the need for a server-side application or database.

  • Map: Leaflet.js with OpenStreetMap and CartoDB tiles.
  • Layout: Bootstrap 5.3 combined with custom responsive CSS.
  • Core data: data.js stores location records, film records and district colours.
  • Extended content: schede.js stores detailed location sections and film_profiles.js stores expanded film profiles.
  • Film collection: films.html and films.js generate the searchable film catalogue and connect each film record to its mapped locations.
  • Narratives: itineraries.html contains the historical timeline and three geographic routes, each supported by a Leaflet map.
  • Theme system: theme.css contains the historical visual variants, while theme.js manages selection, persistence and the movable interface.
  • Shared navigation: navbar.js creates the common navigation and loads the theme system throughout the public site.
  • Linked data: available film records link to Wikidata for external identifiers and further information.

8. Use of Generative AI

Generative AI tools, primarily OpenAI’s ChatGPT and Codex, were used as technical and editorial assistants during the development of Lima Live. Their role was supportive: they helped the authors translate project requirements into code, investigate technical problems, compare possible interface solutions and review the clarity and consistency of the resulting website.

AI assistance was used in the following areas:

  • Technical implementation and debugging: support with HTML, CSS and JavaScript; Leaflet map interactions; dynamic location and film components; responsive layouts; the theme selector; and the location search interface.
  • Repository maintenance and quality control: checking file paths after the repository reorganisation, updating image-format references, identifying syntax or integration errors and comparing related data files for inconsistencies.
  • Design and interaction development: producing exploratory mock-ups, considering alternative homepage and map layouts, and translating the selected editorial periods into interface behaviours and visual treatments.
  • Editorial support: organising sections of the documentation, revising language and helping structure technical explanations. AI tools were also used, where applicable, to help locate or organise potential source material; the external sources themselves, rather than the AI output, remain the references for factual claims.

AI tools were not treated as bibliographic or authoritative sources. The project topic, curatorial scope, selection of films and locations, film–location associations, coordinates, photographs, itinerary structure and final editorial decisions were reviewed and approved by the project authors. AI-assisted outputs were manually inspected, tested and adapted before publication, and final responsibility for the contents and technical decisions remains with Alice Sgarlata and Adriana Villafuerte.

The published website does not connect visitors to a generative AI system and does not generate museum content automatically at runtime. AI was used during the development process only.

9. Sources and Research Method

Film and location records were assembled through project research and compared across structured data, mapping services and historical or cultural sources. Sources do not all provide the same level of detail, so the project distinguishes between confirmed metadata and contextual interpretation.

  • Film metadata: Wikidata and film-specific research for titles, dates, directors, cast, genres and production information.
  • Geographic data: OpenStreetMap, Google Maps and direct location research for names, addresses and coordinates.
  • Historical and interpretive texts: original writing developed for the project from the sources associated with each record.
  • Photography: project images and third-party material. Complete author, URL and licence attribution must be verified before final publication.

Where Wikidata does not contain a film or provides incomplete information, the local record preserves the available researched metadata without inventing an external identifier.

10. Accessibility and Usability

The project uses responsive layouts, semantic headings, alternative text for narrative images, keyboard-accessible controls and visible labels. Text complexity and length can be adjusted independently on location pages. The theme system preserves a readable contrast hierarchy and respects reduced-motion preferences.

Accessibility remains an ongoing process. Image descriptions, keyboard testing, colour contrast and the behaviour of maps and interactive components should be reviewed again before the final release.

11. Known Limitations and Future Development

  • The collection is selective and does not represent the complete history of cinema filmed in Lima.
  • Some films and locations have richer documentation than others because source availability is uneven.
  • Not every location has a complete photograph, extended record or fully verified image attribution.
  • Public transport, opening conditions, accessibility and safety information can change after publication.
  • Some cinematic connections rely on research that still requires a final source-by-source verification.
  • The current interface is in English; a Spanish version would make the project more accessible to local audiences.
  • Future development could add multilingual content, downloadable routes and a more explicit provenance record for each statement and image.
